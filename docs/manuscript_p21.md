# ZeoliteSynthesisBench: a leakage-audited benchmark for zeolite synthesis recipe generation and feasibility prediction

**Author information.** Withheld for double-anonymous review.

## Abstract

Zeolite synthesis relies on experience because gel composition, mineraliser, organic structure-directing agent (OSDA), temperature and time interact nonlinearly. Generative models can propose complete synthesis recipes for a target framework, but current evidence comes from one training set, a single experimental validation and evaluation protocols that are rarely comparable. To keep the split, dataset and metric definitions fixed when comparing models, we present ZeoliteSynthesisBench v1, a leakage-audited benchmark of 24,706 hydrothermal records spanning 3,236 DOIs and the years 1966–2026, with four outcome labels (15,187 positive, 4,478 relative negative, 4,993 absolute negative and 48 no-product), three frozen splits passing six leakage audits, two tasks, conditional recipe generation and feasibility prediction, and seven metric families in one API. Because the ZeoSyn split and ours cannot be merged without train/test leakage, Track A, with 123 systems and 200 samples per system, is the canonical leaderboard, and Track B, with 464 systems from the ZeoSyn split, is reported separately. On Track A, our DDPM baseline FASE-DDPM v0 attains the highest coverage precision, COV-P 0.7359, and TabDDPM-MLP the lowest maximum mean discrepancy, MMD 1.3261. Condition consistency is 0.1532 for FASE-DDPM v0 and 0.1672 for v0.5, against 0.8730 for real test-side recipes, which locates the main gap on the generation side. The benchmark supplies the data card, split manifests, metric code, baselines and a static leaderboard, so that generators and feasibility predictors can be compared on the same audited splits.

**Keywords:** zeolite synthesis; benchmark; generative modeling; synthesis feasibility; data leakage; hydrothermal synthesis

## 1. Introduction

Zeolites are microporous crystalline aluminosilicates used as catalysts, adsorbents and ion exchangers. Their framework and composition, which underlie these applications, are set during synthesis.

Hydrothermal synthesis is specified by gel composition, mineraliser, organic structure-directing agent (OSDA), temperature and time. These variables interact nonlinearly, and the product is a distribution over framework types and competing phases rather than a single deterministic outcome. No closed-form model maps the synthesis vector to the structure, so recipe design still relies on expert experience and trial-and-error.

Learned mappings have begun to replace this loop. ZeoSyn assembled 23,961 hydrothermal routes and showed that synthesis descriptors rationalize framework formation \[1]. Earlier text-mining and machine-learning work extracted recipes from the literature and related gel composition and conditions to the crystallised product \[2]. These studies show that synthesis–structure relationships are learnable, but they use the relationship to predict products rather than to design recipes.

The next step is to use these relationships to design recipes. For OSDAs, learned zeolite–template relationships have been inverted to propose new templates, and a generative neural network proposed candidate molecules for CHA and SFW \[3]. For gel composition, mineraliser, temperature and time, most learned feature-to-label mappings classify phases or predict properties \[1,2], but two studies have used them to propose conditions or guide crystal engineering. A machine-learning workflow trained on 174 OSDA-free EMT routes generated 6,859 candidate conditions. Six were tested and five gave pure EMT, against a 32% training-set baseline \[4]. A learned composition-to-microstructure map guided the synthesis of high-purity FAU at Si/Al 3.5, above the previous direct-synthesis limit of about 2.8 \[5]. Both focus on a single framework family, and neither generates a complete recipe. Generative models are trained mainly on successful routes, and failed syntheses are not used as negatives for a target framework. Zeolite-specific generative models remain data-limited. Even the largest training set contains only tens of thousands of recipes \[1,6].

ZeoSyn, the largest of these datasets, provides structured gel composition, conditions and outcome labels \[1]. DiffSyn trained a conditional diffusion model on it and generated complete recipes for target products. One generated recipe was experimentally validated on UFI at Si/Al 19.0 \[6]. Together they provide the data and the generation baseline for a recipe-level benchmark, but the validation remains a single-system case.

No shared, leakage-audited evaluation substrate yet exists. Public datasets differ in schema, label semantics and splitting, and reported numbers are rarely comparable across protocols \[15]. Negative outcomes are underreported and inconsistently labelled. We present ZeoliteSynthesisBench v1, a leakage-audited benchmark built on 24,706 hydrothermal records with four outcome labels, three frozen splits and seven metric families. The benchmark keeps our project split and the ZeoSyn split \[1] separate, so that neither is used to rank the other. It evaluates conditional recipe generation and synthesis-feasibility prediction under one label schema, and scores generated and real recipes with the same condition-consistency model. It also reports the generation–reality gap explicitly. Condition consistency reaches 0.1532 for FASE-DDPM v0 and 0.1672 for v0.5, against 0.8730 for real test-side recipes, while physical validity is 0.947–0.992 for every learned sampler.

The benchmark contributes a leakage-audited dataset of 24,706 hydrothermal records with four outcome labels, a frozen 149-field schema, per-route provenance and a data card that states known biases. Three splits are frozen, and six audits cover DOI purity, (framework, DOI) group purity, held-out-framework absence, time-boundary monotonicity, increment-DOI disjointness and JZO placement. The protocol is dual-track, keeping the ZeoSyn split \[1] and our project split separate, and it defines seven metric families with explicit aggregation rules and five Track A baselines with a static leaderboard and bit-for-bit manifests.

The work draws on zeolite synthesis records, literature mining and high-throughput synthesis \[1,2,7], OSDA design \[3], machine-learning-guided synthesis \[4,5], generative models for inorganic materials and recipes \[6,8–12], data cards, leakage studies and benchmark practice \[13–15], and feasibility prediction with failed syntheses \[16]. These references define the data sources, the modeling backbones and the audit rules used below.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Data

The dataset contains 24,706 hydrothermal records, each with 149 fields. It was assembled from 23,961 source routes of ZeoSyn \[1]. Four literature increments added 428 rows, and seven rolling extraction batches added 173 net rows. An upgrade step contributed another 144 rows. The records span 3,236 DOIs and publication years from 1966 to 2026. Compositions are stored as normalised element mole fractions. Temperatures are recorded in °C, and times are recorded in hours. OSDA molecules carry 444 RDKit descriptors and an availability flag. Each route carries provenance fields for the source table, DOI, batch and extraction flags. The records come from the literature, and high-throughput synthesis platforms provide complementary recipe data \[7]. Figure 1 summarizes the flow from the records to the two tracks and the feasibility domain.

Table 1 defines four outcome labels. The feasibility task uses `xtal`, the union of positive and relative\_neg, as the headline label. It uses `pure`, which contains positive records only, as a reference. Negative labels are heterogeneous by definition. A failed attempt may fail because of an unrecorded variable rather than because the recipe is intrinsically infeasible. The data card states this distinction, and the benchmark does not relabel these rows.

**Table 1. Outcome labels.**

| Label          | Definition                                       |      n |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | -----: |
| `positive`     | target framework as the only crystalline product | 15,187 |
| `relative_neg` | target with a secondary crystalline phase        |  4,478 |
| `absolute_neg` | amorphous, dense, layered or unknown product     |  4,993 |
| `no_product`   | no solid product reported                        |     48 |

The feasibility domain is built from the labelled records and 308 literature-failure rows. Duplicates are removed using the deduplication manifest, and Tier-A duplicates are exact or near-exact repeats listed in that manifest. The 901 removed rows are 616 xtal-positive and 285 xtal-negative. After removing them and adding the 308 literature-failure rows, the domain contains 24,113 records, of which 19,049 are xtal-positive and 5,064 are negative. Here xtal-positive combines `positive` and `relative_neg`, and xtal-negative combines `absolute_neg` and `no_product`. The domain has 490 features. Of these, 444 are standardised OSDA descriptors. The remaining 46 comprise 36 log-mole, ratio and condition columns, nine extended ratio and charge-balance columns and one OSDA-presence flag.

The benchmark freezes three splits of the labelled records with split seed 42. The `random` split divides DOIs 80/10/10, which leaves 19,870 training, 2,426 validation and 2,410 test rows. The `heldout` split excludes LTL, SAS, ITG, RTH, SZR and LTA from training, giving 21,616 training and 3,067 test rows. Another 23 rows of held-out frameworks share a DOI with training rows and are excluded. The `time_2018` split trains on records published in or before 2018, giving 22,067 training and 2,379 test rows. The remaining 260 rows have no usable year and are excluded. Six audits pass. They check same-DOI overlap and framework–DOI group overlap. They also verify that held-out frameworks do not appear in training and that increment DOIs do not intersect the base v1.0 release. The time boundary is monotonic at both registered cutoffs, 2020 and 2018, and the released split uses 2018. All 32 rows of the JZO framework increment fall on the time-test side. The audit follows data-card and leakage guidance \[13,14]. The JZO framework increment therefore provides a natural time-held-out test.

The dataset contains 15,187 positive records. Most routes do not report a seed. We read non-reporting as unseeded. The `random` test split contains only 443 xtal-negative rows, which limits statistical power, and the data card flags this imbalance. Literature coverage is biased toward open-access sources. The data card records this bias.

### 2.2 Benchmark protocol

ZeoSyn \[1] defines its own system split, and DiffSyn \[6] publishes predictions for 464 test systems from that split. Our system split is constructed independently. An audit shows that those predictions cover only 26 of our 123 Track A systems. Another 97 Track A systems fall inside the ZeoSyn training and validation range, so loading the released DiffSyn weights and re-inferring would constitute training-set leakage. Conversely, 350 of the 464 ZeoSyn systems fall inside our training set. The two tracks therefore cannot be merged, ranked together or averaged. Track A is the canonical leaderboard, and Track B reports the baselines published by DiffSyn \[6] separately, without cross-track inference. Figure 2 summarizes the overlap.

![Figure 2](../assets/p21_f2_dual_track_300dpi.png)

**Figure 2. Two tracks cannot be merged.** Of the 123 Track A systems, 26 are covered by DiffSyn predictions and 97 fall inside the ZeoSyn train/val range. Of the 464 Track B systems, 350 fall inside our training set and 114 do not. Values from the released split manifest and benchmark audit report.

Track A is a separate system split used for the generation leaderboard, and the three splits in 2.1 are used to score the feasibility task. Track A contains 123 systems that have at least five real test rows and a framework label other than Dense or Amorphous. The five-row minimum gives each system enough real test rows for the condition-consistency reference, and Dense or Amorphous labels are excluded because they are not target frameworks. Training support is not constrained and is analysed in 3.2.

The recipe generator is trained on a 21,714-row subset of the 24,706 records. The subset excludes the 48 `no_product` rows, records whose framework code is mixed or absent from the released descriptor table, and records whose OSDA is not mapped to a released descriptor, which leaves 87.9% of the corpus. This subset is split at the system level with seed 0 into 16,058 training, 2,294 validation and 3,362 test rows. Track A selects its 123 systems from the test side and evaluates them on 2,087 of the 3,362 real test rows. Each system contributes 200 generated samples, the truncation used in DiffSyn's coverage evaluation \[6]. Conditioning uses the system's 143-dimensional framework descriptor and 14-dimensional OSDA descriptor, so that all methods receive the same target. Diffusion models use a classifier-free guidance scale of `cond_scale = 0.75`, fixed for all diffusion baselines so that conditioning strength is not a free parameter. The model seed is 42. The FASE-DDPM baseline reuses the frozen baseline parquet files. Evaluation is deterministic. The convex-hull-outside test is solved over the full generated pool of 24,600 rows, 123 systems × 200 samples, without subsampling. Groups with a degenerate linear program use the registered per-column box fallback, so the reported fraction is a lower bound.

The benchmark defines two tasks. The generation task takes a target `zeo`–`osda` pair and asks for 200 twelve-dimensional recipes. Each recipe contains `Si/Al`, `Al/P`, `Si/Ge`, `Si/B`, `Na/T`, `K/T`, `OH/T`, `F/T`, `H2O/T`, `sda1/T`, `cryst_temp` and `cryst_time`. The feasibility task takes a recipe and asks for the probability that the system is synthesisable. The benchmark supplies the test systems and metrics but does not retrain the classifier. The label library contains 4,478 relative-negative rows, 4,993 absolute-negative rows and 48 no-product rows. Under the `xtal` label, relative-negative rows count as positive. The negative side is `absolute_neg` plus `no_product`, or 5,041 rows before deduplication. The feasibility domain adds 308 literature-failure rows to that side, as described in 2.1. The data card accompanies these rows. The additional literature-failure and increment rows enter the training side only and are excluded from the three test sets.

Submissions are UTF-8 CSV files. Each file contains the 12 recipe columns and the `zeo` and `osda` columns. Each submission contains exactly 200 rows per system. Submissions do not include test ground truth. Each submission declares the method name and the training data. It also states whether test-system information was used. The submission records the seed and the dependency versions. Code is released under MIT, and data are released under CC BY 4.0. The data card contains the DOI lists and extraction rules.

### 2.3 Metrics

All seven metric families are implemented once in `wp5_metrics_api_v0.ZeoSynMetrics`. Table 2 lists them.

**Table 2. Metric families.**

| # | Metric                      | Definition                                                                                                                      | Aggregation                                          |
| - | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| ① | W2 joint / per-dimension W1 | ZeoSyn/DiffSyn `scale_x_syn_ratio` max-normalised population space / raw units                                              | per-system mean                                      |
| ② | MMD                         | same normalised space                                                                                                           | per-system mean                                      |
| ③ | COV-P / COV-R               | raw units, prediction set fixed at 200                                                                                          | per-system mean                                      |
| ④ | physical validity           | strict hydrothermal window `[20, 350] °C`, `0 < t ≤ 20,000 h`                                                                   | global pool and per-system mean, reported separately |
| ⑤ | condition consistency (CC)  | the frozen v0.5 scorer maps 12 columns to a `zeo_key` softmax head and treats `frac@0.5` as primary and `frac@0.2` as auxiliary | per-system mean                                      |
| ⑥ | novelty                     | global NN, skeleton-level NN, skeleton-level hull-outside, global box-outside                                                   | global pool                                          |
| ⑦ | extrapolation               | framework training rows: rich (≥50), mid (5–49), few (<5)                                                                        | stratified mean                                      |

W2 is the Wasserstein-2 distance [17], and MMD is the maximum mean discrepancy [18]. Both are computed in the `scale_x_syn_ratio` max-normalised space used by ZeoSyn \[1] and DiffSyn \[6]. W1 is the mean of the 12 per-dimension Wasserstein-1 distances in raw units. Coverage uses the metric of Naeem et al. [19]. COV-P is precision and COV-R is recall under this metric. Both are computed in raw units, with the prediction set fixed at 200. Physical validity (PV) counts recipes that fall inside the strict hydrothermal window of 20–350 °C with `0 < t ≤ 20,000 h`. Condition consistency uses the frozen v0.5 scorer, a 198-class head over the 12 recipe columns that is trained on the population and predicts `zeo_key`. Mean P is the mean predicted probability assigned to the target `zeo_key`. `frac@τ` is the fraction of recipes for which the predicted probability of the target `zeo_key` is at least τ. The primary reading is `frac@0.5`, and `frac@0.2` is auxiliary. Top-1 is the fraction of recipes for which the scorer's most likely `zeo_key` equals the target. The released metric code contains the scorer and the `frac@τ` implementation. Novelty reports NN global and NN skel., the Euclidean nearest-neighbour distances in the ZeoSyn/DiffSyn normalised 12-column recipe space against the global training pool and against training rows with the same skeleton, where skeleton denotes the framework type (`zeo_key`). It also reports Hull-out, the fraction of generated recipes outside the convex hull of the training recipes in that space, and Box-out, the fraction outside the per-column training range. All novelty metrics are computed over the global pool. Extrapolation is a stratified mean over systems grouped by training support. A system has rich support when its framework has at least 50 training rows, mid support when the framework has 5 to 49 training rows, and few support when the framework has fewer than 5.

Feasibility readings use three frozen heads and are not retrained. L1 is a 198-class phase-classification head scored by top-1 accuracy. L2 is a binary feasibility head scored by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) on `xtal` and `pure`. L3 is a purity/competition head scored by top-1 accuracy for multiclass output and by accuracy for the EMT binary output. The three heads are trained on different label domains, with 13,286 rows for L1, 24,113 for L2 and 17,364 for L3, so their test sizes differ. Calibration is reported as `ECE_post`, the expected calibration error after temperature scaling \[20]. Section 3.5 scores these heads against the frozen deployment bundle. The deployment bundle, its calibration temperatures and the per-arm test sizes are documented in the Supporting Information.

Each generation headline reports both the per-system mean and the stratified result. Global and per-system physical validity are kept separate. Each feasibility reading names the head and the split. Any statement about the best method names the metric and the track.

### 2.5 Reproducibility

Data are released as `zeobench_db_v1_8.parquet` and `zeobench_splits_v1_8.parquet`. The database file contains 24,706 rows and 149 columns. The manifest records md5 values for both files. The benchmark runs in two stages. `wp5_bench_v1_selfbaselines.py` produces the baseline samples. `wp5_bench_v1_table.py` computes the metrics. It writes the CSV and Markdown outputs and the static leaderboard. Re-running is idempotent. The `--from-csv` option regenerates tables without recomputation.

The FASE-DDPM baseline reproduces the G3 frozen readings bit-for-bit. The readings are COV-P 0.7359, W2 0.3583, PV 0.9867, CC 0.1532, skeleton NN 0.0402 and hull-outside 0.7378. These readings match Table 4. The model seed is fixed at 42. The convex-hull linear program is solved over the full generated pool of 24,600 rows, with the registered fallback for degenerate groups. Evaluation uses no subsampling. Every table records the method and the data version. It also records the code md5, the model seed and the environment.

The evaluation separates recipe generation from feasibility prediction. Track A supplies the generation leaderboard, and the three frozen splits supply the feasibility readings. ### 3.2 Coverage under support

Coverage under support asks whether COV-P depends on how much training data the target framework has. Support is defined by the number of real training rows that carry the same framework as the target system. A system is rich when its framework has at least 50 training rows, mid at 5 to 49 rows and few below 5 rows. Training-support counts are computed from the real rows on the Track A training side, and each system is assigned to one stratum by the count for its framework. The three strata contain 96, 9 and 18 systems. Per-system COV-P is computed by the coverage function from the generated rows and the real test rows for that system, then averaged over systems rather than rows. The same 200 generated samples per system enter every stratum, so differences across strata are not caused by unequal generation budgets.

COV-P falls from rich to mid to few for FASE-DDPM v0 (0.7604, 0.6779 and 0.6345), TabDDPM-MLP (0.6738, 0.5812 and 0.5699) and KDE-QT (0.6090, 0.5420 and 0.4934). FASE-DDPM v0.5 gives 0.7468, 0.6712 and 0.6816, so its few stratum sits slightly above mid. Figure 3 shows the five methods across the three strata.

![Figure 3](../assets/p21_f3_stratified_covp_300dpi.png)

**Figure 3. Coverage precision by support stratum.** COV-P for five Track A methods across rich (n = 96 systems), mid (n = 9 systems) and few (n = 18 systems) framework-level training-support strata. Values from the per-system Track A result table.

The system-count-weighted mean of the three strata is 0.7359, matching the Table 4 COV-P. The decline is consistent across FASE-DDPM v0, TabDDPM-MLP and KDE-QT, and it is largest between rich and mid. The v0.5 inversion in the few stratum is small and rests on 9 mid and 18 few systems, so it is an instability rather than evidence that less data help. The rich stratum is not saturated, which means that support remains a limiting factor even where training data are most abundant. Random-Uniform is included as the floor and is not interpreted stratum by stratum.

### 3.3 Physical validity and condition consistency

Physical validity and condition consistency ask different questions about the same generated rows. Physical validity checks whether a recipe falls inside the strict hydrothermal window of 20–350 °C and has a crystallization time in (0, 20,000] h. Condition consistency checks whether the generated recipe matches the target framework. PV is computed row by row from `cryst_temp` and `cryst_time`, and a row counts as valid when both conditions hold. The CC reading uses the frozen 198-class scorer, with CC\@0.5 as the primary value and CC\@0.2 as auxiliary. The scorer takes the `zeo_key` softmax and reads the probability assigned to the target key. CC\@0.5 counts a row as consistent when that probability reaches 0.5, and CC\@0.2 uses 0.2. The real-row reference is scored with the same scorer and 12-column preprocessing on 2,087 real test rows, the test side of the 123 Track A systems. It is an in-sample ceiling rather than an independent human baseline.

Every learned sampler exceeds 0.94 PV, while Random-Uniform falls to 0.5507. Physical plausibility therefore does not separate the learned samplers. On condition consistency, FASE-DDPM v0 reaches 0.1532 and v0.5 reaches 0.1672, while the real-row reference reaches 0.8730, with mean P 0.8337, the mean predicted probability of the target, and top-1 0.9109. Figure 4 places the five methods against the real-row value.

![Figure 4](../assets/p21_f4_cc_gap_300dpi.png)

**Figure 4. Generation–reality gap in condition consistency.** CC\@0.5 for the five methods against the real-row reference 0.8730 (n = 2,087), with the ratio of each method's CC\@0.5 to the real-row value annotated. Values from the Track A result table and the real-row reference (`Data/wp5_g3_formal_report.txt`).

The PV result shows that the generated recipes are mostly inside the hydrothermal window. The CC result shows that they rarely carry the target framework. Because the same scorer produces both the generated-row and real-row readings, the gap is not a difference between two annotators. The scorer's in-sample error is 12.7%, so a generator that reproduced real recipes exactly would reach 0.8730, and generated-row CC is not corrected for this. The remaining gap is consistent with a generation-side limitation, and we draw no causal conclusion beyond that.

### 3.4 Novelty and skeleton coverage

Novelty asks whether the generated recipes are close copies of training rows or new combinations. The reading uses nearest-neighbour distance in the ZeoSyn/DiffSyn normalised 12-column recipe space and the fraction of generated recipes outside the training convex hull. Each generated row is compared with the real training rows in that space. NN global is the Euclidean distance to the nearest training row. NN skel. repeats the search within training rows that share the target skeleton. Hull-outside solves the convex-hull membership test on the training rows and records the fraction of generated rows outside. The seven systems without skeleton training rows are excluded from NN skel. and recorded as outside for hull-outside, following the benchmark definition.

FASE-DDPM v0 has NN skel. 0.0402 and a hull-outside fraction of 0.7378, meaning 73.78% of generated rows fall outside the training hull, against 1.5841 and 1.0000 (100%) for Random-Uniform. Seven eligible systems, covering 1,400 generated rows, have no training rows for their skeleton. Their skeleton-level nearest-neighbour distance is undefined, and their hull-outside value follows the definition above. We report this gap rather than impute a nearest-neighbour value.

The hull-outside fraction includes the 1,400 rows from the seven unsupported systems, which are outside by definition, so part of the gap is definitional rather than measured. The combination of low NN skel. and high hull-outside still shows that the generated recipes sit near individual training rows while most of them fall outside the training hull. Proximity to a training row therefore does not imply interpolation. Novelty is not a substitute for physical validity or condition consistency, and the benchmark reports it alongside those readings rather than as a standalone score.

### 3.5 Feasibility benchmark

The feasibility benchmark asks whether frozen classifiers transfer across the random, heldout and time splits. The benchmark does not retrain a classifier. It scores the frozen deployment bundle against the three splits, and the readings are the comparison point for submissions. The feasibility readings are produced by evaluating the frozen deployment bundle on the real test rows of each split. No classifier is refit, and no calibration parameter is re-estimated on the test split. L1, L2 and L3 are scored separately. AUROC is computed from the continuous scores, top-1 from the argmax class, and accuracy from the binary EMT output. ECE_post applies the calibration temperature recorded with each referenceable head and bins the calibrated probabilities before averaging the bin gaps. Table 5 restates the feasibility readings in `Data/feasibility_scorer_v1_2_report.txt` and the calibration readings in `Data/m10_calib_v1_report.txt`. L1 is a 198-class phase classifier, L2 is the feasibility head with `xtal` as the primary label and `pure` as the reference, and L3 is the purity/competition head with multiclass and EMT binary outputs. Failed experiments can carry signal when used as negative labels \[16], which motivates the label library but does not turn every failed recipe into evidence of infeasibility.

The calibration residual follows the same pattern, so the heldout weakness is not removed by post-hoc scaling. The readings are descriptive and do not support a claim about model quality.

### 3.6 Scope and limitations

The benchmark reports relative order under this protocol. It does not establish that any method is state of the art. Seed robustness is not measurable here, because training uses a single seed and is deterministic. A resampling diagnostic puts the evaluation-side bootstrap half-width at 2.88 percentage points, above the ±1.68 percentage point band from a 10-seed subsample check (sd 0.86), so evaluation-side resampling dominates. Neither substitutes for multi-seed training. No recipe is validated experimentally.

Track A contains the 123 test-side systems with at least five real test rows. Eighteen of these systems target frameworks with fewer than five training rows. They form the few-support stratum in 3.2, and their readings are correspondingly unstable. The baselines published by DiffSyn \[6] cannot be ranked against Track A, and cross-method comparison on Track A is limited to the self-implemented baselines. Track B contains 464 systems from the ZeoSyn split. Of these, 372 pass the released feature filter and enter the metric table (SI Table S-B1), and their values are reported on their own.

The condition-consistency scorer is a 198-class head trained on the population. Real-row CC is 0.8730, so scorer error is 12.7 percentage points, and generated-row CC is not corrected for this.

## 4. Conclusions

The benchmark provides a leakage-audited dataset, three frozen splits with six executed audits, a dual-track protocol that keeps the ZeoSyn split \[1] and our project split separate, seven metric families in one API, and five Track A baselines with a static leaderboard. Under this protocol no single method dominates. FASE-DDPM v0 leads coverage precision (0.7359), TabDDPM-MLP leads MMD (1.3261), and all learned samplers remain physically valid (0.947–0.992). Condition consistency is the largest deficiency, at 0.1532–0.1672 for the strongest generators against 0.8730 for the real-row reference. No result reported here is experimentally validated. The substrate, its audits and its stated limits are released together, so that generators and feasibility predictors can be compared on the same audited splits.
